# Experience maps, journey maps, service blueprints

Three different artifacts that get confused. Pick by what you need to see.

| Artifact | Shows | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **Experience map** | The customer's end-to-end experience of getting a job done, product-agnostic | Early discovery. You do not yet know where you fit |
| **Journey map** | The customer's path through your product or service, stage by stage | You know the flow and need to find where it breaks |
| **Service blueprint** | The journey plus everything behind the counter that makes it happen | Operational or service products where failure is backstage |

The most common mistake is drawing a journey map when you needed an experience map, which
locks the analysis inside your existing product before you have understood the job.

---

## Experience map

**Build it from interview stories.** One map per participant first, then merge. Merging too
early averages away the variation, and the variation is usually the finding.

**Rows:**
- Phases, in the customer's terms, not yours
- Actions: what they actually do
- Artefacts: what they use. Spreadsheets, notebooks, WhatsApp groups, second screens
- People: who else is involved
- Thinking: in their words, with quotes and locators
- Pain and friction, with severity from the evidence not from your judgement
- Workarounds, marked. Every workaround is a need somebody already paid to solve

**Practice from Torres:** draw the map from your assumptions before the first interview,
then correct it after each one. The corrections are the learning, and the exercise makes
your assumptions visible before the data can be bent to fit them.

**Quality bar:** every cell traces to a source. Cells with no source are marked as
assumptions, and there will be more of them than you expect.

---

## Journey map

**Stages:** awareness, consideration, signup, first use, habit, expansion, renewal or
churn. Adapt to the product.

**Per stage:**
- Goal in the customer's terms
- Actions
- Touchpoints
- Emotional state, evidenced not imagined
- Drop-off rate from analytics, with n
- Known friction, with sources
- Open questions

**Pair it with the funnel.** A journey map without the quantitative drop-off at each stage
is a story; the funnel without the journey is a set of numbers with no explanation. The
value is in the two together: the funnel says where, the map says why.

**Quality bar:** every emotional claim has a source. "Frustrated" with nothing behind it is
the most common piece of fiction in journey maps.

---

## Service blueprint

Shostack (HBR, 1984) introduced the blueprint, the line of visibility and fail points. The
version below, with five lanes and three lines, is Bitner, Ostrom and Morgan (2008),
developed from it. Eight rows:

1. Physical evidence
2. Customer actions
3. **Line of interaction**
4. Frontstage: what staff or system does visibly
5. **Line of visibility**
6. Backstage: what happens out of sight
7. **Line of internal interaction**
8. Support processes and systems

**Use when** the customer-visible failure has a backstage cause: delivery, healthcare,
government services, logistics, anything with an operations team.

**What it reveals that a journey map cannot:** the handoffs. Most service failures happen
at a line, not inside a lane. Mark every crossing and check what can go wrong there.

---

## Merging maps across participants

1. Keep individual maps intact and referenced
2. Merge only where the sequence genuinely matches
3. Where paths diverge, that is a segment boundary. Show both, do not average
4. Mark frequency: how many participants took each path, out of how many
5. Keep the outliers visible on the map rather than in an appendix. The outlier is often
   the early signal of a segment you have not named yet
