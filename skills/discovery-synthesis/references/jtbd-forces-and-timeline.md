# JTBD forces and timeline analysis

For analysing switch interviews. Sources: Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek's switch interview
method; Christensen et al., *Competing Against Luck* (2016); Moesta, *Demand-Side Sales
101* (2020).

---

## The timeline

Reconstruct backwards in the interview, present forwards in the analysis.

```
FIRST THOUGHT ──► PASSIVE LOOKING ──► EVENT ──► ACTIVE LOOKING ──► DECIDING ──► USING
     |                  |               |            |               |            |
  something         aware but      something     comparing       committing    reality
  is not right      not urgent     forces it     options         to one        versus
                                                                              expectation
```

**The three moments that matter most:**

1. **First thought.** Often months or years before the purchase. Tells you what the real
   trigger is, which is almost never what marketing assumes.
2. **The event that ended passive looking.** The specific thing that made it urgent. This is
   the highest-leverage finding in the whole method, because it tells you when to be
   present.
3. **The gap between first thought and active looking.** What kept them in the old way.
   That is the habit force, and it is what your product has to overcome.

**Record real dates where possible.** "About three months before" is enough. The intervals
tell you the sales cycle and the decay of the trigger.

## The four forces

Two push toward change, two hold in place. Change happens only when push plus pull exceeds
habit plus anxiety.

| Force | Direction | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **Push** of the situation | Toward change | What was going wrong. The cost of staying |
| **Pull** of the new solution | Toward change | What they imagined would be better. Note this is imagined, not experienced |
| **Habit** of the present | Against change | Sunk investment, familiarity, "it mostly works", other people's dependence on it |
| **Anxiety** about the new | Against change | Risk of it not working, migration cost, learning cost, being blamed if it fails |

**The asymmetry teams miss.** Marketing spends almost all its effort on pull. Most failed
adoptions fail on anxiety and habit, which pull cannot fix. If your win rate is low and
prospects say the product looks great, the blockers are on the right-hand side.

**Design implications by force:**

| Force | Product response |
|---|---|
| Weak push | You are targeting people without the problem. Change the segment, not the product |
| Weak pull | The value is not legible. Usually a communication problem, sometimes a real one |
| Strong habit | Reduce switching cost: import, parallel running, keep their existing artefacts working |
| Strong anxiety | Reduce risk: trial, guarantee, references, reversibility, doing the migration for them, someone to blame other than the buyer |

## Analysing across interviews

1. Build one timeline per participant
2. Align them on the event that ended passive looking, not on the purchase date
3. Look for a common trigger class. If one exists, it is the single most actionable finding
   in the study, because it tells you exactly when to appear
4. Tabulate forces across participants: which recur, which are unique
5. Note the anxieties that were never resolved, only tolerated. Those are the churn risk

**On non-switchers.** Run the same interview with people who considered you and stayed put.
The forces that held them are your real competition. This interview is worth three of the
switcher kind and almost nobody does it.

## Job statement

Written after the analysis, not before.

> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

**Rules:** no product in it, no technology, and it should still be true in ten years. If
your product disappeared tomorrow the job would remain, which is the test of whether you
have written a job or a feature.

Ulwick's outcome statements are the quantifiable complement: direction + metric + object +
context. "Minimise the time it takes to reconcile cash against orders at end of day." Those
can be rated for importance and satisfaction in a survey, which is what makes ODI
opportunity scoring possible.
