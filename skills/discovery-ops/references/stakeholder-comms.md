# Stakeholder communication

A finding that does not reach the person making the decision has not been produced.

---

## The two-sentence format

Most findings need to travel in two sentences. Practise writing them.

> Merchants stall at adding their first product, not at signup: nine of eleven described
> giving up on photos [interviews, Mar 2026]. That means the signup simplification on the
> roadmap will not move time-to-first-order, and we should test bulk photo upload instead.

Sentence one: the finding and its evidence. Sentence two: what it changes. If sentence two
is hard to write, the finding may not be decision-relevant, which is worth knowing before
you write six pages.

---

## The weekly digest

Short, same format every week, published whether or not there is news.

```
Discovery, week of 14 April

Talked to: 3 merchants (2 new, 1 churned). P24, P25, P26.
Learned: churned merchant P26 left because stock counts drifted, not price. Third
  mention of drift. Now the second-strongest opportunity under time-to-first-order.
Running: bulk upload fake door, day 9 of 14. Reads Friday against a 12% threshold.
Changed: added opportunity "cannot trust the stock number". Removed "wants a
  desktop version", no evidence in 14 interviews.
Next week: 2 sessions booked with merchants who signed up and never listed anything.
```

Same shape every week. It is a habit signal as much as an information channel, and
consistency is what makes it read.

## The readout

Structure in `../product-discovery/references/06-artifacts.md`. The rules that matter for
stakeholders:

- **The answer is in the first paragraph.** Nobody reads to the conclusion
- Confidence level attached to every claim, using the four words
- Sample and frame in one sentence, near the top, not in an appendix
- Disconfirming evidence in its own section, never omitted
- Recommendation with the trade-off named
- What we still do not know, ranked

**Do not present findings as slides without keeping the readout as the source of truth.**
Slides lose provenance, and a slide with a number and no source is what ends up in a board
pack a year later.

## When a stakeholder disagrees with the evidence

Common, and usually not really about the evidence.

**1. Find out what would change their mind.** "What would you need to see?" If there is no
answer, the disagreement is not about evidence and no amount more will help.

**2. Check whether they are right.** Sometimes they are. They may know something about
constraints, history, or the account that is not in your data. Ask what they know that you
do not. This question resolves more disagreements than any argument.

**3. Separate the finding from the recommendation.** People often accept the finding and
disagree with what to do about it. That is a legitimate and different conversation, and
conflating the two makes both harder.

**4. Offer the cheapest decisive test.** "We disagree about whether merchants will pay. A
two-week pre-sale to twenty merchants would settle it. Shall we?" Converting a disagreement
into a test is the whole job.

**5. If they overrule, record it.** Neutrally, without commentary, in the decision record:
the decision, the decider, the evidence available at the time, and the confidence level.
Not to be proved right later, but so that the eventual post-mortem is honest about what was
known when.

## When there is no evidence yet

Do not fill the gap with confident-sounding analysis. Say:

> We do not know this yet. Here is what we would need, here is the cheapest way to get it,
> here is how long it takes, and here is what I would do in the meantime given the risk.

Then give a recommendation anyway, labelled as judgement rather than evidence. Refusing to
have a view is not rigour, it is abdication. Having a view and labelling it correctly is the
job.

## Making discovery visible

- Invite stakeholders to **watch** a session, not to read about it. One session changes more
  minds than ten readouts. Brief them: observers do not speak
- Keep the opportunity solution tree visible somewhere the team passes
- Share the ruled-out list as prominently as the findings. It is the clearest evidence that
  discovery saves money
- Report the cost avoided: "we did not build X, which was six weeks, because the test came
  back at 3 percent against a 12 percent threshold." That sentence is what buys the practice
  its next quarter
