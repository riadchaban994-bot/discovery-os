# Moderator errors

Twelve failures, what they cost, and how to recover mid-session. Recovery matters: most
guides tell you what not to do and leave you stranded once you have done it.

---

**1. Pitching before the story.**
*Cost:* everything after it is politeness. The session is contaminated.
*Recovery:* accept that solution feedback is now all you can get from this session, and
say so in the snapshot. Do not code anything after the pitch as unprompted need.
*Prevention:* the guide physically puts solution exposure last.

**2. Leading.** "So that was frustrating?"
*Cost:* you receive your own words back, and you will quote them later as theirs.
*Recovery:* "Sorry, let me not put words in your mouth. How would you describe it?"
*Prevention:* strip every adjective from the guide.

**3. Filling silence.**
*Cost:* the second half of the answer, which is where the finding is.
*Recovery:* next time they pause, count three seconds silently.
*Prevention:* write "PAUSE" in the guide margin after the main probes.

**4. Accepting the general answer.** "It is usually a bit of a mess."
*Cost:* an unusable session. Generalities cannot be coded, sized, or designed against.
*Recovery:* "Can you take me to one specific time? The most recent one you remember."
*Prevention:* the story prompt names an activity and a time.

**5. Rescuing during a usability task.**
*Cost:* the finding. Every rescue removes the observation you came for.
*Recovery:* "I am going to stay quiet and see what you would do if I were not here."
*Prevention:* agree the rescue rule before the session and hold to it.

**6. Asking two questions at once.**
*Cost:* they answer the easier one and you lose the other.
*Recovery:* ask the lost one again, alone.

**7. Talking too much.**
*Cost:* proportional. If you spoke half the time, half the session is gone.
*Recovery:* stop mid-sentence, ask "what happened next", and stay quiet.
*Prevention:* watch the talk-time ratio. Under 25 percent moderator is the target.

**8. Interviewing the wrong person.**
*Cost:* the whole session, and it is usually discovered in the first five minutes.
*Recovery:* switch to an expert or referral interview, then ask who actually does the
activity and whether they can introduce you. Salvage a recruit from a lost session.
*Prevention:* screen on recent behaviour, not on role title.

**9. Confirming the hypothesis.**
*Cost:* the study. You will find what you went looking for.
*Recovery:* spend the last ten minutes actively hunting for disconfirmation: "What would
someone who disagrees with all this say? Who do you know who does it completely
differently?"
*Prevention:* write the disconfirming questions into the guide before recruiting.

**10. Taking the feature request literally.** "I want a bulk upload button."
*Cost:* you build the button and the underlying job stays unsolved.
*Recovery:* "Tell me about the last time you needed that. What were you doing?"
*Prevention:* treat every feature request as the visible end of a story and dig for it.

**11. Skipping consent, or rushing it.**
*Cost:* the data may be unusable, and it is a breach of trust regardless.
*Recovery:* stop and get it properly, even if it feels awkward.
*Prevention:* it is written verbatim in the guide, not improvised.

**12. Not writing it up within a day.**
*Cost:* roughly half of what you learned, and all of the texture.
*Recovery:* write it now, and mark what you are unsure of as unsure.
*Prevention:* book 30 minutes immediately after every session, permanently.

---

## Self-check after every session

Five questions, two minutes, before the snapshot:

1. Did I get at least one specific episode with a date, a duration, or a number?
2. What did they say that surprised me? (If nothing, I probably led.)
3. What did I not follow up that I should have?
4. What does this contradict from earlier sessions?
5. What will I ask differently next time?

Question 2 is the quality signal. A session with no surprise is usually a session where the
moderator's assumptions did all the talking.
