# Interview types

Each type answers a different question. Using the wrong one is the most common reason an
interview produces nothing.

---

## 1. Story-based interview (continuous discovery)

**Answers:** what people do, what it costs them, what they need. The default type.

**Structure:**
- Consent and framing, 2 min
- Context: role, situation, what a typical week looks like, 3 min
- Story prompt: "Tell me about the last time you [did the activity]", 20-25 min
- Probing inside the story until the timeline is complete
- Optional solution exposure, last, 10 min
- Close, 3 min

**The prompt matters more than anything else in the guide.** It must name an activity, not
a feeling or an opinion:

| Weak prompt | Strong prompt |
|---|---|
| How do you handle inventory? | Tell me about the last time you ran out of something a customer wanted |
| What are your biggest challenges? | Walk me through yesterday, from opening to closing |
| Do you find the reporting useful? | When did you last need a number you did not have to hand? |

**Cadence.** Torres's argument is that one interview a week, forever, beats fifteen
interviews in one quarter. Continuous interviewing keeps the map current and removes the
recruiting scramble. Weekly is the target; fortnightly is a real practice; quarterly is a
project pretending to be a habit.

## 2. JTBD switch interview

**Answers:** why someone moved from one solution to another, and what nearly stopped them.

**Only valid with people who actually switched**, ideally within the last 90 days. Memory
of the decision decays quickly and reconstructs itself into a tidier story.

**Structure:** work backwards from the moment of purchase or adoption to the first thought.

1. When did you start using it? Where were you? Who else was there?
2. When did you decide? What was the last thing that made it certain?
3. What were you comparing it against?
4. When did you start actively looking? What triggered that?
5. Before that, when did you first think something needed to change?
6. What happened between the first thought and starting to look? Why the gap?

**Then map the four forces:**

| Force | Question |
|---|---|
| Push of the situation | What was going wrong that made staying impossible? |
| Pull of the new | What did you imagine would be better? |
| Habit of the present | What was comfortable about the old way? |
| Anxiety about the new | What worried you? What nearly stopped you? |

Anxiety and habit are where products die, and they are the two forces teams never research.
The design implication is usually to reduce anxiety, not to increase the pull.

**Also run this on people who considered you and did not switch.** That interview is worth
three of the other kind and is almost never done.

## 3. Contextual inquiry / field study

**Answers:** what actually happens, including everything nobody thinks to mention.

**Stance:** master and apprentice. They work, you watch, you ask about what you see as it
happens. You are learning a craft, not conducting an interview.

**Rules:**
- Go where the work is. A conference room is not the field
- Watch the real task, not a demonstration of it
- Ask "what are you doing now" and "why that way", in the moment
- Photograph the workspace with permission. The desk tells you more than the interview
- Note every workaround, every second screen, every piece of paper, every spreadsheet.
  Workarounds are unmet needs that someone has already paid to solve

**Output:** a timeline of the observed session, the artefacts in use, and the interruptions.

## 4. Churn / exit interview

**Answers:** why someone left, and when the decision actually happened.

**The recruiting problem is the whole problem.** Churned users do not owe you a
conversation. Offer a genuine incentive, be honest that you are not selling, keep it to 20
minutes, and reach out from a person rather than a system.

**Structure:**
1. When did you stop using it, roughly?
2. When did you first think about stopping? (Usually months earlier)
3. What were you doing instead by then?
4. What was the specific thing that ended it?
5. What are you using now, and how is that going?
6. What would have had to be true for you to stay?

Question 2 matters most. The cancellation event is the end of the story, not the cause.

**Cancellation-flow surveys capture the last straw and the socially acceptable answer.**
They are a weak instrument and should never be the only one.

## 5. Solution feedback interview (compare and contrast)

**Answers:** which approach connects to a need, and why.

**Rules:**
- Two or three options, equal fidelity, always. Never one
- Counterbalance the order across the sample so the first option does not systematically win
- Collect the story first, in the same session or a previous one
- Ask what they would use it for, not whether they like it
- Watch for the difference between polite interest and "when can I have this"
- Ask what it would replace, and what they would stop doing

**The disqualifying signal.** If they cannot name a specific recent situation where they
would have used it, the enthusiasm is politeness.

## 6. Expert / domain interview

**Answers:** how the domain works, what the constraints are, what the vocabulary is.

Treat as orientation only, L1-L2. Experts describe categories, not your customers, and
consultants selling into the space describe the market they want to exist. Prefer
practitioners who do the work now.

Good uses: regulation, industry workflow, who the real actors are, what has been tried
before and failed, and who you should be talking to instead.

---

## 7. Team and stakeholder interview

**Answers:** what the organisation believes, and where the beliefs conflict.

Run this at the start of any engagement. Ask each stakeholder separately: what does success
look like, what do you believe about customers that others might not, what would make you
change your mind, what has been tried before.

Output is an assumption map of the organisation, labelled `[INTERNAL OPINION]` throughout.
The conflicts you find here are usually the real reason the work is stuck.
