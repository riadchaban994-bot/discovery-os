# Recruiting and sampling

Recruiting is the real constraint on discovery. Every team that interviews continuously has
solved it once and automated it. Every team that interviews rarely re-solves it each time,
which is why they interview rarely.

---

## The sampling frame statement

Every readout contains one sentence naming who could possibly have been in the sample.
Without it, no reader can judge the finding.

> Sample drawn from customers active in the last 30 days who opted into research contact,
> recruited by in-product prompt. Excludes churned users, users on the legacy plan, and
> anyone who has never completed onboarding.

The exclusions are the important half.

## Recruiting channels, ranked by speed

| Channel | Speed | Bias introduced |
|---|---|---|
| In-product prompt on a relevant screen | Fastest | Active, engaged users only |
| Existing research panel or opt-in list | Fast | Repeat participants professionalise |
| Customer success or sales introduction | Fast in B2B | Happy accounts, gatekept |
| Email to a filtered segment | Medium | Email-responsive users |
| Support ticket follow-up | Medium | People with a live problem |
| Recruiting agency or panel | Medium, costs money | Professional respondents, screener gaming |
| Social, community, local networks | Slow | Whoever likes you |
| Intercept in a physical location | Slow | Location and time of day |
| Churned-user outreach | Slowest | Low response, and the ones who reply are unusual |

**The in-product prompt is the highest-leverage thing most teams can build.** A small,
dismissible prompt on the screen where the behaviour happens, offering a short conversation
with an incentive, linked to a booking page. Build it once and recruiting stops being a
project. It also biases toward active users, so keep a second channel for the others.

## Screener design

A screener has one job: exclude people who cannot answer the question. It is not a survey.

**Rules:**
- Screen on **behaviour**, not on attitude or demographics. "Have you shipped an order in
  the last 7 days?" not "Are you interested in logistics?"
- Never reveal what you want. "Do you find inventory management difficult?" recruits people
  who will tell you it is difficult
- Include a disqualifying distractor: a plausible option that, if selected, excludes them.
  Catches people optimising to qualify for the incentive
- Ask for a recent, specific, verifiable behaviour with a date
- Keep it under six questions. Long screeners lose good participants
- In B2B, screen on role in the decision as well as role in the use. Buyer, user, approver
  and blocker are four different interviews

**Example screener, small-retailer study:**

1. Which of these do you do in a typical week? (multi-select, includes distractors, one of
   which disqualifies)
2. In the last 7 days, roughly how many orders did you fulfil? (numeric, range required)
3. Who decides which tools the business uses? (self, someone else, jointly)
4. When did you last change how you track stock? (date, free text)
5. What do you use to track stock today? (free text, no options offered)

Question 5 free text is the real filter. Options prime the answer.

## Sample composition

Design the sample before recruiting, and write it down.

| Consideration | Practice |
|---|---|
| Segments | Interview each separately to saturation. Do not average across segments |
| Extremes | Include heavy users and near-churn users, not only the middle |
| Non-users | The most under-recruited group. If the question is about adoption, non-users are the sample |
| Roles (B2B) | Buyer, user, admin, blocker. Interviewing only the champion gives you the champion's view |
| Geography and language | In multi-market products, findings do not transfer. Sample each market you will act on |
| Accessibility | Include users of assistive technology if the product will be used by them |
| Recency | Recruit on recent behaviour, not on registration date |

## Incentives

- Pay for time, and pay fairly for the local market. An hour of a business owner's time is
  not free anywhere
- Never make the incentive contingent on what they say or on completing "successfully"
- In B2B, individual cash is often against policy. Offer a charity donation, a report of
  the aggregate findings, or early access
- Disclose the incentive in the recruiting message, not after
- Budget roughly: consumer 30-60 minutes at local hourly rates plus a premium for
  scheduling; professionals meaningfully more; senior enterprise buyers rarely accept cash
  and are recruited on relationship and on getting something useful back

---

## Scheduling that does not consume a week

- One booking link with fixed slots, no back-and-forth email
- Recruit a week ahead, always. The habit is what makes it sustainable
- Over-recruit by 30 percent. No-show rates of 20-30 percent are normal for consumer
  research and higher for unpaid sessions
- Confirm 24 hours before with a short human message
- Keep a standing weekly slot in the trio's calendar. If nobody is booked, the slot is used
  for synthesis. Never give the slot back

---

## Data protection

- Collect the minimum. A participant code, a segment, and consent status. Not a name in the
  artifacts
- Store recordings in one controlled place with a retention period and a deletion date
- Separate the identity mapping from the research data
- Tell participants how to withdraw, and honour it including from already-coded data
- In regulated or public-sector contexts, check the local basis for processing before the
  first session, not after
