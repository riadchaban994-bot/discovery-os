# Question bank

Copy, adapt to the domain, cut to fit the time. A guide with more than about fifteen
questions is a survey being read aloud.

---

## Consent and framing (say this, do not improvise it)

> Thanks for the time. I work on [product/area]. I am trying to understand how [activity]
> actually works for you day to day. I am not here to sell you anything and there are no
> right answers. If a question is uncomfortable, skip it, and you can stop at any point.
> I would like to record this so I do not have to take notes and can get your words right.
> The recording stays with the team, is used only for this research, and I can delete it
> and your data afterwards if you ask. Is recording okay?

Wait for an explicit yes. Note the timestamp of consent.

---

## Warm-up

- Tell me about your role. What does a typical week look like?
- How long have you been doing this?
- Who else is involved in [the activity]?
- Walk me through where [the activity] sits in your week.

---

## Story openers

The most important sentence in the guide. Pick one, keep it activity-based.

- Tell me about the last time you [activity].
- Walk me through the most recent time [situation] happened.
- When did you last have to [outcome]? Take me through it.
- Think about the last time [problem] came up. Start from the beginning.
- Tell me about a time this went badly. What happened?
- Tell me about the last time you had to change how you did this.

**If they answer generally,** do not accept it. "That is helpful. Can you take me to one
specific time? The most recent one you remember."

---

## Probes, by what you need

**Sequence**
- What happened next?
- What did you do just before that?
- Then what?
- Where does this start? What kicks it off?

**Detail**
- Walk me through that step by step.
- What did you have open in front of you?
- Who else was involved? What did they do?
- How long did that take?
- How often does that happen?
- When was the last time before this one?

**Cost and consequence**
- What did that cost you? Time, money, or something else?
- What happened as a result?
- What would have happened if you had not caught it?
- Who else was affected?

**Workarounds (the highest-yield probe in the set)**
- How do you handle that today?
- Is there anything you do to work around it?
- Has anyone on the team built something for this?
- Can you show me?

**Emotion and importance, without asking about importance**
- How did you feel when that happened?
- Was that a normal day or an unusual one?
- What did you do about it afterwards?
- Have you tried to fix this? What happened?
- Have you looked for a tool for this? What did you find?

The last two are the best proxies for importance that exist in an interview. People who
have already searched for a solution have revealed how much the problem matters. Asking
"how important is this" gets a number that means nothing.

**Silence.** Three full seconds after they stop. The second half of the answer is where the
finding is. Interviewers fill silence because it is uncomfortable; that discomfort costs
more findings than any other single habit.

---

## Solution exposure (last section, optional)

- Here are two approaches. Take a look and think out loud.
- Which of these, if either, fits how you work? Why?
- What would you use this for? Take me to a specific situation.
- What would this replace?
- What is missing that would stop you using it?
- What is confusing about it?

**Never ask:** Do you like it? Would you use it? How much would you pay? Is this useful?

---

## Closing

- What have I not asked about that I should have?
- What is the hardest part of [activity] that we have not talked about?
- Who else does this differently that I should talk to?
- Would you be open to a follow-up in a few months?

The third question is the recruiting engine. Ask it every time.

---

## Banned question forms

| Banned | Why | Replacement |
|---|---|---|
| Would you use...? | Predicts nothing. L2 at best | When did you last need something like this? |
| How much would you pay? | People do not know | What do you spend on this today? What is it costing you? |
| Do you like...? | Politeness, not evidence | What would you use it for? |
| How important is X, 1 to 5? | A number with no anchor | Have you ever tried to solve X? What happened? |
| What features do you want? | Asks them to do your job | What is the last thing that went wrong? |
| Don't you think...? | Leading | What do you think? |
| How frustrating is X? | Presupposes frustration | Walk me through the last time X happened |
| Would it be helpful if...? | Yes. It always gets a yes | What would you do differently if that existed? |
| Do you ever...? | Yes/no, kills the story | Tell me about the last time you... |
| How often do you...? early on | Estimated, unreliable | When was the last time? And the time before? |

The last one is worth its own note: asking for a frequency gets an estimate; asking for the
last two occurrences gets you a real interval you can compute.

## Adapting across languages and cultures

- Interview in the participant's strongest language, always, even if analysis is in another
- Translate the guide, then back-translate it, then check the back-translation. Concepts
  like "frustrating", "convenient" and "trust" travel badly
- Indirect cultures answer criticism obliquely. "It was fine" from someone who then
  describes three workarounds is not fine. Follow the behaviour, not the adjective
- In high-deference contexts, the interviewer's seniority and gender change the answers.
  Name it in the sampling-frame statement and consider who moderates
- Hospitality norms in many markets make participants agreeable. Weight stated positives
  down and observed behaviour up
- Never use a colleague of the participant as an interpreter. Use a neutral one, brief
  them on not summarising, and ask for full translation rather than paraphrase
